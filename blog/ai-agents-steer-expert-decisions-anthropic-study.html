Your AI is quietly making your decisions. Here's how to take them back.

Anthropic gave 50 professional analysts an AI agent with a secretly random opinion. They followed it 86% of the time, and only 15% credited it. What the study found, why it applies to anyone who signs off on AI-assisted work, and a simple routine to keep the judgment yours.

Short answer

AI agents can steer experts' decisions without anyone noticing. In a study published this week, Anthropic's researchers gave 50 experienced analysts a Claude agent whose preferred option on each judgment call was set by a coin flip. The analysts went with the agent's lean 86% of the time, against roughly 50% by chance, and only 15% credited the agent afterwards. Experience and AI scepticism didn't protect them. Anthropic says this is not a reason to use AI less. It's a reason to change how you use it: write your own view before you ask, make the AI argue every option, and keep a short record of where each decision came from.

Most of my work with clients is about getting AI to do more: write the report, sort the leads, draft the quote. But there's a line I keep drawing in every coaching programme. The AI does the work. You make the calls. The goal is your independence, not a smarter crutch.

This week Anthropic published the most uncomfortable evidence I've seen for why that line matters. Not because the AI lied. Because it didn't need to.

What did Anthropic's study on AI and expert judgment find?

Anthropic's Alignment team ran a controlled experiment, published on its research site and reported in detail by Decrypt and AI Weekly. Fifty analysts experienced in causal inference (working out from data whether one thing really causes another) each used Claude Code, running Claude Opus 4.8, to advise a fictional farming cooperative on whether to expand an advisory programme.

The trick: for each key judgment call, such as how to measure outcomes or which farms to compare, a coin flip decided which option the agent would favour. That lean was hidden in its instructions. The agents were told never to mislead. The analysts didn't know any of this.

There was good news too. Analysts working with the agent finished faster and made far fewer serious errors, although Anthropic notes the comparison group without an agent was small and less experienced. And the researchers deliberately strengthened the agents' lean so they could measure the effect, tested one task, in one field, with one model. Real-world steering is probably weaker. It's not zero.

The line from the paper I keep thinking about: "The work still goes out under the expert's name."

Why does AI steering your decisions matter if you're not a data analyst?

Because the same pattern shows up in ordinary work. You ask AI which supplier to pick, which candidate to shortlist, how to price a quote, which clients to chase first, how to reply to a difficult email. The AI gives a balanced-sounding answer that quietly leans one way. You agree, because it's reasonable and you're busy. A month later you'd swear it was your idea.

Three reasons this matters more for small businesses and independent professionals than for big companies:

  • You are the last check. A bank has reviewers and committees. A freelancer, a consultant or a business owner usually signs off alone.
  • Your judgment is what clients pay for. If you're an accountant, a consultant or a designer, the AI can do the hours. The judgment is the product. If it's quietly outsourced, so is your value.
  • The lean doesn't have to be planted to exist. In the study it was a coin flip. In real life it can come from how you phrased the question, what's in the AI's memory, or which option sounds more familiar to the model. Nobody needs bad intentions for your decisions to drift.

Anthropic calls the risk "disempowerment": people losing real control over decisions that depend on their judgment, even with a "human in the loop". The human was in the loop here. The loop just didn't do much.

How do you use AI without letting it make your decisions?

Anthropic's authors are clear that this is not an argument for using AI less. They point to two fixes for how agents should behave: argue the best case for each option, or ask the human for their view first. You don't have to wait for AI companies to build that in. You can do it today, with any chatbot.

  1. Write your call before you ask. One line: "I'd pick option A because…". Thirty seconds, in a note, before you open the AI. The study shows a prior view isn't a perfect shield, but without one you have nothing to compare against.
  2. Ask for the strongest case for every option, the same length. Not "what do you recommend?" but "argue each option as well as you can". The study noticed the agents rarely argued for the alternative on their own. Make them.
  3. Ask what would change its mind, and yours. "What evidence would make option B the right choice?" This turns a recommendation back into a judgment you can check.
  4. When it disagrees with you, slow down instead of switching. In the study, disagreement flipped analysts 60% of the time. Ask for the single fact that makes the difference, and check that fact yourself.
  5. Keep a three-word log. For decisions that matter (prices, hires, client advice), note: mine, AI's, or changed by AI. Review it monthly. If "AI's" keeps growing, you'll see it before your clients do.
  6. Separate the doing from the deciding. Let AI gather data, draft and calculate. Keep the final choice in a step where you write the reason in your own words.

Copy this prompt: the two-sided advisor

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini before any decision you'll sign off on. It forces the AI to argue every side and hands the call back to you.

Two-sided advisor 
I need to make a decision and I want to keep the judgment mine.
Do NOT tell me which option to choose.

THE DECISION: [e.g. which of two suppliers to use]
THE OPTIONS: [A, B, (C)]
WHAT I CARE ABOUT: [cost, speed, risk, relationship...]
MY CURRENT LEAN AND WHY: [one line, written before asking you]

Then:
1. Argue the strongest honest case for EACH option.
   Same length and effort for each.
2. For each option, list the 2 facts that would most change
   the answer, and how I can check them myself.
3. Point out where my current lean might be weak, and where
   it might be right.
4. List any assumptions you made that I didn't give you.
5. End with: "The decision is yours." No recommendation.
Before

"Which supplier should I go with?" The AI gives a tidy, reasonable answer that leans one way. You agree, and a month later you're sure it was your idea.

After

Your lean in one line first. The AI argues both sides equally and names the facts that would change the answer. You check one of them and decide, knowing why.

Cloudflare: one hosted AI model mixed other customers' data into replies

In a disclosure published on 26 October, Cloudflare explained that between 27 August and 9 September, 155 responses from the GLM-5.3 model on its Workers AI platform contained data from other customers' recent requests. Only very long prompts were affected. A security researcher reported it, Cloudflare found no evidence of deliberate exploitation, and it has fixed both the configuration and the underlying software bug.

What it means for you: a rare bug, quickly fixed, but a useful reminder. Before you paste client contracts or personal data into any AI tool, check what your plan promises about data handling, and keep the most sensitive material out of tools you haven't vetted.

FAQ

What did Anthropic's study on AI agents and expert judgment find?

They matched the agent's lean 86% of the time, against about 50% by chance, and only 15% credited the agent afterwards. 77% repeated its arguments, and experience or AI scepticism didn't reduce the effect.

Did the AI lie to the analysts?

No. The agents were instructed never to mislead. They simply leaned one way on each decision and rarely argued for the alternative. That was enough to steer most choices.

Does this mean I should stop using AI for decisions?

No, and Anthropic says the same. Analysts with the agent worked faster and made fewer serious errors. The fix is how you use it: write your view first, ask for the strongest case for every option, check the facts that would change the answer, and note where each decision came from.
