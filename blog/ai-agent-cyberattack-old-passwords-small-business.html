Hackers just sent AI agents after 200 organisations. Old passwords let them in.

CrowdStrike says a China-linked group used a team of AI agents to attack more than 200 organisations in 11 days. The AI was new. The way in was not: keys nobody changed, passwords that could be guessed, secrets left in email. What happened, why it matters even if you run a five-person business, and a one-week checklist to close the same doors.

Short answer

Attackers are now using teams of AI agents to break into many organisations at once. On 29 October, CrowdStrike reported that a China-linked group it calls VAPOR PANDA used an AI attack framework called ARTEX, running mainly on DeepSeek, against more than 200 organisations in at least 14 countries over 11 days, starting 17 October. CrowdStrike confirmed the attackers got inside nine of them. The targets were mostly governments and large companies, but the doors the agents walked through are the same ones most small businesses leave open: old keys never changed, guessable passwords and secrets sitting in email. AI makes those mistakes cheaper to find, at scale.

When I set up AI for a client, the first hour is rarely about AI. It's about passwords. Who has the login to the website, which shared account three ex-employees still know, where the Wi-Fi and bank details are written down. Usually the answer is "in an email somewhere".

This week, a security report showed why that hour matters more than ever. Attackers handed the boring work of trying every door to AI agents, and the agents kept trying, in parallel, across hundreds of targets.

What happened in the VAPOR PANDA AI cyberattack?

In a report published on 29 October, CrowdStrike describes the first time it has seen this group use AI-driven intrusion tools. In its words: "This campaign marks an escalation in China-nexus use of AI agents."

How did the AI agents actually get in?

This is the part worth reading twice. CrowdStrike's examples aren't science fiction. They're old, ordinary mistakes, found faster:

  • Keys nobody changed. At a Greek government body, the operator gave the agent a mail-server directory export from 2022 containing authentication keys that were likely never rotated. The agent used them to forge an administrator login and read mailboxes.
  • Secrets left in email. In the same case, the agent then found VPN settings in a user's sent mail and combined them with a VPN key the operator said came from a May 2024 email leak, to reach the internal network.
  • Guessable passwords. Password-spraying (trying common passwords across many accounts) hit government, think-tank and company accounts.
  • Weak login checks and unpatched services. At two US pharmaceutical companies, the agents exploited a login system that accepted unsigned credentials and a service that required no authentication at all.
  • Computers without protection. At one company, the operator told the agents to look for Windows machines that didn't have CrowdStrike's security software installed.

Why does an AI cyberattack on governments matter to a small business?

Because the economics just changed. Until now, a careful attacker had to choose targets: there's only so much time to read old leaks, guess passwords and poke at servers. CrowdStrike's point is that agentic tools remove that limit. As the report puts it, ARTEX "enables this scale of expansion at speed."

That matters to you in three ways:

  • Being small is less of a hiding place. When trying a door costs almost nothing, there's little reason to skip the small doors. This campaign targeted big organisations, but criminals copy what works, and CrowdStrike notes it has already seen ARTEX-type tools used by financially motivated groups.
  • Your old leaks still count. The way into a government body came from a 2022 export and a 2024 leak. If a password of yours appeared in a breach years ago and you never changed it, that's the same door.
  • Your inbox is a map. If you've ever emailed yourself a password, a Wi-Fi key or bank login, an agent that gets into your email will find it in seconds.

The good news: none of the fixes need a security team. They need a week of boring discipline. If you're wondering what an "agent framework" like ARTEX is, my explainer on what an agent harness is covers the same idea from the useful side.

How do you protect your business from AI-powered hackers?

Here's the one-week version I'd give any freelancer or small team, based on CrowdStrike's recommendations translated for a business without an IT department:

  1. Day 1: list your keys. Write down every account that would hurt if someone else got in: email, website, domain, bank, payments, cloud storage, social accounts, accounting software. Note who has access.
  2. Day 2: check for old leaks. Put your work email addresses into a breach-checking service such as Have I Been Pwned. Any account tied to a breach gets a new password, even if the breach was years ago.
  3. Day 3: two-step login everywhere. Turn on two-step verification (an authenticator app, not SMS where you can avoid it) on every account from Day 1. Email first, because email resets everything else.
  4. Day 4: get a password manager. One unique password per account, stored in a manager, shared with staff through the manager, not WhatsApp or email.
  5. Day 5: clean your inbox. Search your email for "password", "login", "VPN", "PIN" and "IBAN". Move anything sensitive into the password manager and delete the emails.
  6. Day 6: remove old access. Ex-employees, old freelancers, agencies you no longer use: remove them, and change any shared passwords they knew.
  7. Day 7: updates and protection. Turn on automatic updates on every laptop, phone and website plugin. Make sure every computer has up-to-date security software. If someone manages a server or website for you, ask them when it was last patched and when the keys were last changed.

OpenAI rewrites the rulebook for how ChatGPT behaves

On 29 October, OpenAI published an updated Model Spec, the document that sets out how its models should behave. According to this daily digest, it now hangs off five values: faithfulness to human intent, harm minimisation, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care. It adds guidance on context, oversight and agency, clarifies how conflicts between platform, developer and user instructions get resolved, and removes much of the detailed style guidance.

What it means for you: the direction is models that use judgment when your instructions run out, rather than following them to the letter. Write instructions that explain your goal and why, not just the steps. That gets better results from any model, and it fits how OpenAI now says its models should read you.

FAQ

What is VAPOR PANDA?

VAPOR PANDA is the name CrowdStrike uses for a China-nexus hacking group that has targeted governments, think tanks, energy and extractive organisations in Europe, North America, Oceania and the UK since 2021. On 29 October 2026, CrowdStrike reported its first observed use of AI-driven intrusion tools.

What is ARTEX?

ARTEX is an agentic AI intrusion framework. According to CrowdStrike, a main agent coordinates planner and worker agents over a shared graph of facts, findings and goals, moving each attack from initial access through reconnaissance to lateral movement. In this campaign it ran mainly on DeepSeek plus a second, unidentified commercial model.

How many organisations did the AI-driven attack hit?

CrowdStrike says more than 200 organisations in at least 14 countries were targeted over an 11-day window starting 17 October 2026. It confirmed post-exploitation activity at nine organisations and password-spraying or credential scanning against at least 12 more.

Are small businesses at risk from AI-powered hacking?

This campaign targeted mostly governments and large companies. But the methods (unchanged old keys, guessable passwords, secrets in email, unpatched systems) are common in small businesses, and AI agents make trying many targets cheap. CrowdStrike notes similar frameworks have already been used by financially motivated groups.

How can I protect my business from AI cyberattacks?

Change passwords and keys after any leak, including old ones; turn on two-step login on every important account, starting with email; use a password manager; remove passwords and bank details from your inbox; remove access for ex-staff and old suppliers; and keep automatic updates and security software on every device.

Is it safe to paste passwords into ChatGPT or Claude to get security help?

No. Describe your setup in general terms (which tools you use, who has access, whether two-step login is on) and let the AI build a plan. Never paste real passwords, keys or bank details into any AI chat.
