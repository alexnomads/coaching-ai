ChatGPT is moving into your back office. Is your business ready?

Oracle and OpenAI announced on 27 October that ChatGPT will plug into Oracle's business software, including NetSuite, with a free-to-start bundle for small businesses. Here's what was actually announced, what's still missing, and the five things I'd write down before letting any AI near your books.

Short answer

Oracle is putting ChatGPT and Codex inside its business software. A new Oracle AI Workspace lets employees use ChatGPT to look up data and run approved workflows in Oracle Fusion Applications and NetSuite. App Composer lets non-programmers build internal apps with Codex. For small businesses there's an "Oracle AI SMB Bundle": ChatGPT Business, the Workspace, Codex, NetSuite QuickStart for invoicing and cash flow, and $12,000 of Oracle cloud credits a year, described as free to start. No launch dates, eligibility rules or full terms have been published yet. Don't switch software for it. Use the wait to get your data, categories and approval rules in order, because that's what decides whether any AI can run your back office.

In almost every first session with a business owner, there's a moment where I connect their AI to the software that runs the business and ask it a simple question. "Which clients owe us money?" "Where did our leads come from this month?"

The answer is never the problem. The data behind it is. Jobs with no category. Two spreadsheets that disagree. A discount policy that only exists in the owner's head. The AI does exactly what it's told, and that's when everyone discovers nobody ever told it, or anyone else, how the business really works.

That's why yesterday's announcement from Oracle and OpenAI matters more than the headline suggests. Chat is becoming the front door to business software. Whether that door opens onto a tidy office or a messy one is up to you.

What did Oracle and OpenAI announce?

At Oracle AI World in Las Vegas on 27 October, Oracle announced a set of products that combine OpenAI's models, ChatGPT and Codex with Oracle's applications and cloud. According to Oracle's press release, there are four pieces:

Oracle also said its cloud will offer Google's TPU chips, OpenAI's own inference chip and Cerebras systems, as reported by Data Studios. That's the infrastructure story. For a business owner, the interesting part is the software.

Small Business Trends makes the right point about the bundle: it doesn't say whether every service in it is covered by the credits, so confirm costs, usage limits and eligibility with Oracle before you plan around it.

Why does ChatGPT inside business software matter to small businesses?

Because it's the same move everyone is making. Microsoft has Copilot inside Office. Google pitched its Gemini agent working inside Gmail, Sheets and business tools earlier this month. Now ChatGPT is going inside an ERP and accounting suite. Within a year, most software you pay for will have a chat box that can read your data and, with permission, act on it.

Three things follow from that:

  • The chat answer is only as good as the records behind it. If half your jobs have no client type, "Which kind of client is most profitable?" gets a confident, wrong answer.
  • "Approved workflows" have to be approved by someone. Oracle stresses governance and approvals. That only works if you've decided who can approve what, and up to which amount.
  • Bundles create lock-in. $12,000 of credits a year is real money. It's also how platforms get you to move your invoicing, cash flow and internal apps into one place. That can be fine. Just decide it on purpose.

Is your business data ready for AI?

Three moments from my own coaching sessions, all anonymised, all things I see again and again.

With an electrical contractor in Sydney, the first test of his AI accounts chat surfaced an overdue payment nobody had flagged. Good news. Then we tried to report on job types and found most jobs had no client category. We had to write a categorisation rulebook before the AI could answer anything useful.

With an events production company in Italy, we built an agent that turns a site-visit transcript into a client quote. It couldn't apply discounts, because the discount policy had never been written down.

With a real-estate agency in Dubai, a campaign to their existing contacts waited until the CRM was cleaned. Running AI on messy data just sends the mess faster.

None of these were AI problems. They were "the business never wrote its own rules" problems. AI just made them visible. Every new back-office AI, from Oracle or anyone else, will hit the same wall.

How to get your business ready for back-office AI

  1. Don't switch software for a launch announcement. If you already run NetSuite, watch for the Workspace rollout. If you don't, check first whether your current accounting or job-management tool already connects to ChatGPT or Claude. A connector to the system you have usually beats a migration.
  2. Write down your five weekly questions. The things you actually check: who owes us money, which jobs ran over, where leads came from, what's due this week, cash in the bank versus bills due. These become your first AI tests.
  3. Fix categories before you ask questions. Pick 5 to 8 client or job types, write one line defining each, and apply them to the last three months of records. An afternoon of this beats weeks of wrong answers.
  4. Write your approval rules. Who can approve a quote, a refund, a supplier payment, and up to what amount. One page. This is what "approved workflows" will need, in any tool.
  5. Start read-only. For the first month, the AI looks and reports. It doesn't send, pay, change or delete. Check its answers against what you know.
  6. Keep your rules in files you own. Your categories, approval rules and weekly questions should live in a document you control, so you can take them to the next tool. That's your exit plan.

Copy this prompt: your back-office AI readiness check

Paste this into ChatGPT or Claude. It interviews you and hands back the rulebook any back-office AI will need, whichever software you end up using.

Back-office AI readiness check 
Act as an operations consultant helping a small business
get ready to connect AI to its accounting and admin software.

MY BUSINESS: [what we sell, team size, country]
SOFTWARE WE USE: [accounting, CRM, job management, spreadsheets]
WHERE THINGS GET MESSY: [e.g. jobs without categories,
  quotes in WhatsApp, two spreadsheets for the same thing]

Interview me one question at a time (max 12 questions), then write:
1. MY 5 WEEKLY QUESTIONS: what I should be able to ask an AI
   about my numbers every week, in plain words.
2. CATEGORY RULEBOOK: 5-8 client/job/expense categories,
   one-line definition each, and how to handle edge cases.
3. APPROVAL RULES: who approves quotes, refunds, discounts and
   supplier payments, and the limits for each.
4. READ-ONLY TESTS: 5 questions to test an AI on my data, with
   how I can check each answer myself in 2 minutes.
5. DATA GAPS: what's missing or inconsistent today, ranked by
   how much it would mislead an AI.
6. NEVER WITHOUT ME: the actions an AI must never take without
   my explicit approval.

Keep it to two pages. Plain English, no jargon.
Before

"ChatGPT can read our accounts now, so it'll tell us what's going on." It does, confidently, using categories nobody applied and rules nobody wrote.

After

Five weekly questions, a one-page category rulebook, written approval limits, and a month of read-only answers you've checked. Then you let it act.

Google DeepMind's Genie 4 generates interactive worlds, now with sound

Google DeepMind announced Genie 4, a "world model" that generates explorable, interactive scenes from a prompt. TechCrunch reports it now creates matching audio (music, ambient sound, dialogue), lets you change the scene mid-session by typing, and keeps a world consistent for up to two minutes, double Genie 3. What it means for you: not a business tool yet. But testers see it for storyboarding and prototyping. Hex raises $100M and rebuilds its analytics tool for AI agents

Data analytics company Hex announced a $100 million round at a $1.2 billion valuation, led by Peak XV and 137 Ventures, and Hex 4.0, a from-scratch rebuild for humans and AI agents working on data together. In its announcement, Hex says agents wrote much of the new code. For non-technical users, the notable feature is that any number in a report can be traced back to its logic and data source, with a signal for whether a figure rests on a trusted definition or needs a data team's review. It's in private beta.

How to use it: borrow the idea even if you never use Hex. When an AI gives you a number about your business, ask it to show where the number came from and how it was calculated. If it can't, don't make a decision on it.
