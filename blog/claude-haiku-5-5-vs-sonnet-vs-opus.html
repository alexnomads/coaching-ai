Claude Haiku 5.5 vs Sonnet 5.5 vs Opus 5.5: 40× cheaper isn't 40× worse

Someone spent $700+ running the same prompts through all three models. Here's what the results mean for which model you should use for your own work, and how to stop overpaying for AI.

Short answer

Use Haiku 5.5 for high-volume work (summaries, sorting, extracting data, first drafts), Sonnet 5.5 for building apps and most everyday work, and Opus 5.5 for planning and anything visual or complex. Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens, 40× less than Opus 5.5 ($4). In an 11-test comparison it matched the bigger models on text tasks, won one build outright, and lost mainly on visual quality. The best setup is both: Opus plans, Haiku does the work.

What do the results actually mean?

  1. Text work is saturated. On the fraud audit (massive spreadsheets, one clear answer) all three models found the same eight schemes. For reading, summarising, sorting and drafting, the cheapest model is now good enough most of the time.
  2. Opus still owns visual and complex builds. 3D worlds, detailed websites, responsive game controls: the gap shows up the moment something has to look good and work across many moving parts.
  3. Sonnet and Opus are closer than you think. Sonnet made the better finance app and the better animated video. If you default to the biggest model "just in case", you're often paying for nothing.
  4. The quality gap is nowhere near 40×. Opus's builds were better, but not 40 times better. That changes what's affordable: work you'd never run on Opus because of cost becomes cheap enough to try.
The question isn't "which model is best?" It's "which model is best for this step?"

Plan with Opus, execute with Haiku: the pattern to copy

The most useful idea from the test is about combining models, not choosing one. A frontier model (Opus or Sonnet) does the thinking: it breaks the job down and decides what to do. Haiku is the hands. It runs the repetitive steps, many times, for almost nothing.

Jobs to hand to Haiku 5.5:

  • Summarising reports, call transcripts, long email threads.
  • Classifying support tickets, leads, invoices, CVs.
  • Querying a database or spreadsheet and returning the answer.
  • Extracting one number or field from thousands of rows, like the fraud audit.
  • Research sweeps: reading 50 sources and pulling out what matters, so the bigger model only sees the summary.

Here's how it looks in real businesses. An events company gets hundreds of supplier emails and quotes a month: Haiku reads and tags each one, and the expensive model only steps in to draft the client proposal. A recruiter screens 300 CVs against a brief: Haiku scores each one, Opus writes the shortlist rationale for the top ten. Same quality where it matters, a fraction of the bill everywhere else.

If this sounds like a map of jobs and handoffs, it is. I call it graph engineering: you design the work first, then give each step to the cheapest model that can do it well.

The 100k token cliff: how to keep Haiku cheap

Under 100k tokens$0.10 in / $0.50 out
Over 100k (the cliff)$0.50 in / $2.50 out
Cache reads$0.01 in / $0.05 out

Once a Haiku 5.5 conversation passes 100,000 tokens, the price jumps 5×. But when you keep asking questions about the same material in one session, the repeated part is read from cache at a tenth of the price.

So the rule is simple:

  1. Keep each Haiku thread short. One task, one batch of material.
  2. Reuse the same context for follow-up questions so you benefit from cache reads.
  3. Cut the thread at about 100k tokens. Have the bigger model summarise what Haiku produced, then start a fresh Haiku thread with that summary.

You don't have to manage this by hand. Tell your orchestrating agent the rule once and it will do it for you (there's a prompt for that below).

  • In a chat app: switch model per conversation. Use the small one for summaries and sorting, and switch up when the work gets complex.

FAQ

Is Claude Haiku 5.5 good enough to replace Opus?

For text work like summarising, classifying, extracting data and drafting, mostly yes: in an 11-test comparison all three models scored 8/8 on a fraud audit. For visual, design-heavy or complex multi-step builds, Opus 5.5 is still clearly better.

How much cheaper is Haiku 5.5 than Opus 5.5?

Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens. Opus 5.5 costs $4 and $20, so Haiku is 40× cheaper. Above 100,000 tokens in one conversation, Haiku's price rises 5× to $0.50 and $2.50.

What is the Haiku 5.5 100k token price cliff?

Below 100,000 tokens, Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 in and $0.50 out per million tokens. Above that it costs $0.50 and $2.50. Cache reads cost $0.01 and $0.05, so the cheapest approach is short threads that reuse the same context, restarted with a summary at around 100k tokens.

Is Sonnet 5.5 better than Opus 5.5?

Not overall, but closer than most people expect. Sonnet 5.5 beat Opus 5.5 on the personal finance app and the animated video tests, at half the price per token. Opus won most visual and game builds.

What is the best way to combine Claude models?

Let a frontier model (Opus or Sonnet) plan the work and break it into steps, then hand the repetitive steps (summaries, classification, data extraction, research sweeps) to Haiku 5.5 as sub-agents.

Which Claude model should a small business use?

Start with Sonnet 5.5 for everyday work, move high-volume tasks such as email sorting and document summaries to Haiku 5.5, and keep Opus 5.5 for planning, strategy and anything visual.
