The best AI agents still fail 4 in 10 everyday tasks. Test yours on your own mess.

ShellBench, a new benchmark published on 24 November, gave about a dozen AI models 100 ordinary jobs inside messy inboxes, spreadsheets and receipts. The top score was 62.5%. Here's what went wrong, why it matters if you're handing admin to an agent, and a simple test to run before you do.

Short answer

ShellBench, built by the OpenClaw Foundation with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, tested AI agents on 100 everyday tasks in realistic, messy workspaces. The best models passed only about 6 in 10. The top model, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, averaged 62.5% across five agent setups, and passed all three repeat runs of a task only 48.8% of the time. Half of the failures came from the agent leaving something out, usually information it had already found. 15.7% of runs broke at least one safety rule, and 40.3% of attempts on one task recommended duplicate or incorrect invoice payments. The lesson for business owners: an agent is only as reliable as the files you give it, so test it on your own messy work, more than once, before you let it act alone.

I'll start with a disclosure. I teach people to set up AI agents, including OpenClaw, the open-source agent whose foundation built this benchmark. So I opened the results expecting a sales pitch. It reads more like a warning label.

The tasks aren't coding puzzles or exam questions. They're the jobs people actually hand to a personal agent: plan a dinner, sort the receipts, arrange a school pickup, pay the invoices. The kind of work most of my clients want off their plate first.

What is ShellBench?

According to TechCrunch and Techstrong.ai, ShellBench is a benchmark for everyday personal-agent work, designed from more than 10 million anonymised real-world agent interactions. The full results are on OpenClaw's blog, and a research paper is due later.

QuestionWhat the reports say
What was tested?100 tasks in simulated workspaces full of emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, receipts and PDFs. Most include outdated records, conflicting instructions or potentially malicious requests.
Which AI?About a dozen models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, DeepSeek and Moonshot AI (Techstrong counts 12, The Deep View 13), run inside five agent "harnesses": OpenClaw, Claude Code, Codex CLI, Hermes and Pi. 53,100 runs in total.
How was it scored?90 tasks were graded against 721 criteria, 381 of them about safety. To pass, an agent had to meet every required criterion. The other 10 tasks were checked with code.
Who did best?GPT-6 Astra 62.5%, Claude Fable 5.2 61.9%, GPT-6.1 Sol 61.4%, averaged across harnesses.
How consistent?GPT-6 Astra completed 75.1% of tasks at least once in three tries, but all three times on only 48.8%.
How safe?15.7% of all runs failed at least one safety check. Even among runs that got everything else right, 9.9% failed a safety requirement.
What's the caveat?OpenClaw built the benchmark and is also one of the harnesses tested. Each run was graded once, and the detailed paper isn't out yet.

Why do AI agents fail at everyday tasks?

Mostly not because they're "not smart enough". The most useful number comes from The Deep View: 51% of the failures to complete a task involved the agent leaving information out. In 87% of those cases, the agent had already collected the information it needed. It found it, then didn't use it.

Techstrong gives a clear example. On a dinner-planning task, agents picked the right restaurant in 522 of 531 attempts. But only 152 attempts met every requirement, because many forgot to point to where the group's budget or schedule came from. The answer looked right. The work wasn't finished.

The money examples are the ones I'd print out. TechCrunch reports that 40.3% of attempts on one task recommended duplicate or incorrect invoice payments, 18.6% failed to spot and hold back a sensitive bank upload token, and 79.5% didn't properly account for a child's school pickup. The safety tests covered phishing, fraudulent payment requests, exposing sensitive information and irreversible actions.

None of this surprises me. A real-estate agent in Dubai I coach ran leads and listings in several spreadsheets with an AI workspace on top. The sheets and the dashboards were out of sync. Whatever the AI answered, it could only be as right as the sheet it happened to read. We spent one session getting the data to agree before doing anything clever with AI. ShellBench is that lesson, measured 53,100 times.

Why it matters to you

  • "It worked when I tried it" isn't a test. A task the best model gets right once and wrong the next time is a task you still need to check. Run it more than once before you trust it.
  • Your mess is the real exam. Old price lists, duplicate invoices, two versions of the same schedule. The agents in ShellBench struggled exactly where real businesses are messiest.
  • Money and personal data need a human yes. If 4 in 10 attempts on one task recommended a wrong or duplicate payment, no agent should pay a bill without your approval. I made the same point about ChatGPT for Accounting: start read-only.
  • The cheaper model may be enough. Techstrong reports GPT-6 Astra cost about $1.10 per attempt and GPT-6.1 Sol about $0.19 (The Deep View says $1.01 and 17 cents), for roughly a one-point difference in pass rate. That fits what I wrote in my GPT-6.2 post.
  • Pick the model first, then the tool. Techstrong reports a median gap of 45.7 points between the best and worst models inside the same harness, versus 6.2 points for the same model across harnesses.

How do I test an AI agent before trusting it with my admin?

  1. Pick one real, boring task. Matching receipts to card payments, drafting replies to supplier emails, preparing the weekly invoice run. Not a demo task: one you do every week.
  2. Use your real files, mess included. Leave in the old price list and the duplicate invoice. If the agent only works on tidy data, you need to know now.
  3. Run it three times. Same task, same files, fresh chat each time. If the three answers disagree on anything that matters, it isn't ready to run alone.
  4. Ask it to show its sources. For every figure, date or name, the agent should say which file it came from. ShellBench's most common failure was leaving out what it had already found; asking for sources makes that visible.
  5. Keep a human yes on anything irreversible. Payments, sending emails to clients, deleting files, sharing personal data. Draft, then you approve. I explained how to set those limits in this access check.
  6. Fix the source, not the prompt. When the agent gets something wrong because two files disagree, decide which one is the truth and archive the other. That fix helps every future task, not just this one.

Copy this prompt: run a three-pass agent check

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini with your files attached. Run it three times in fresh chats and compare the three tables.

Three-pass agent check 
You're helping me test whether you can handle this task reliably before I let an AI agent do it on its own.

Task: [describe the task, e.g. "check this month's supplier invoices against our purchase orders and list what should be paid"]

Files attached: [list them]

Rules:
1. Before answering, list every file you used and anything in the files that looks outdated, duplicated or contradictory.
2. For every figure, date or name in your answer, say which file it came from.
3. If two files disagree, don't pick one silently. Flag it and tell me what you'd need to decide.
4. Do not take any action. Draft only.
5. End with a table: item | your recommendation | source file | confidence (high/medium/low) | needs my check (yes/no).

Then list anything you were asked to consider but left out of your answer, and why.
Before

Try the agent once on a tidy example, see a confident answer, and let it run the weekly invoice batch on its own from Monday.

After

Run it three times on last month's real files, compare the tables, fix the files that disagree, and keep payments as drafts until the three runs match.

The UK warns AI is speeding up attacks on old weaknesses

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says it has seen a "marked rise" in AI-driven cyberattacks in recent months, TechCrunch reports. Since September, several incidents involving autonomous hacking tools caused intrusions and downtime. None of the weaknesses were new: exposed internet-facing systems, weak or guessable passwords and abused developer API endpoints. What changed is the speed. The NCSC's message: "building strong foundations has never mattered more."

How to use it: this week, turn on two-factor login for email, banking and your accounting tool, and change any password you've reused. I covered the same pattern in old passwords let AI agents in.

Google's AI Mode can now read your Wallet, with Drive next

Google expanded Personal Intelligence in Search's AI Mode to Google Wallet, with Google Drive and the Gemini app to follow, TechCrunch and 9to5Google report. Drive lets it take Docs, Sheets and PDFs into account. Google says Wallet access is read-only: AI Mode can't move, manage or invest money. It already connects to Gmail, Calendar and Photos. Connecting is optional and can be switched off.

What it means for you: before you connect a work Drive, decide which folders hold client or financial data. You can review the connection in your Google activity settings and turn personalisation off in Search settings.

Anthropic's Claude Ship lets agents approve code changes

Anthropic launched Claude Ship for Team and Enterprise customers using GitHub, according to Sam on AI, citing TechCrunch. It bundles Code Review with two beta agents: one fixes failing checks and review feedback, the other approves changes that a team's plain-language policy allows. Anthropic suggests starting in observe-only mode, on one repository, with monthly spending limits.

What it means for you: even the company selling the agent recommends watching it before letting it act. Copy that rollout for any agent you adopt: observe first, one area, a spending cap.
