Fixing other people's AI work now costs almost a day a week

A new study commissioned by Arden University, a UK university, says workers who clean up colleagues' AI output spend close to 7 hours a week on it, and more than half of staff don't tell their boss they use AI at all. Here's what the numbers say, why "AI slop" is a setup problem rather than an AI problem, and the five rules that stop it in a small team.

Short answer

A survey of 2,000 working adults, commissioned by Arden University and reported again by The Express this weekend, found that 46% of workers correct work colleagues produced with AI, with an average of 6 hours 57 minutes a week spent correcting it. Two in ten completely redo it, and 53% of employees don't tell their supervisor they use AI. Earlier studies from Workday and Zapier point the same way: AI saves time, then a big share of that time goes on rework. The fix isn't banning AI. It's giving it your context, agreeing who checks what, and making people say when AI was used.

One of my clients, an operations director at a UK construction contractor, came to his first lesson after roughly 11 hours and more than 200 prompts spent trying to get AI to fix a single near-miss safety form. Every version looked polished. None of them was right.

The fix wasn't a better prompt. It was a workspace with memory, his house rules and his real documents. That gap between "looks finished" and "is right" is exactly what this week's study is measuring, and it's why I think it's the most useful AI story of the weekend for anyone who manages people or works in a team.

Is AI slop a new problem?

No. It's the third study this year with the same shape. In January, Semafor reported a Workday study finding that most people save 1 to 7 hours a week with AI, and nearly 40% of that saved time goes on rewriting, fact-checking and correcting. A fuller write-up of the same study says only 14% of employees consistently get net-positive results from AI.

The same month, ITPro covered a Zapier survey of more than 1,100 US enterprise AI users: about four and a half hours a week revising or redoing AI output, and only 2% never need to revise it. The detail I find most telling: untrained workers were six times more likely to say AI makes them less productive.

Put together, the message is clear. AI isn't failing. Untrained, unchecked, context-free AI is creating a second job for the person downstream. I saw the same thing from another angle last week with the benchmark where the best model passed only 12% of real expert work: give AI a whole job and you get confident half-work.

AI slop isn't an AI problem. It's an onboarding problem with a deadline.

Why it matters to you

  • If you run a team, you're probably paying for this already. 7 hours is close to a working day. If even two people on a small team are cleaning up others' AI work, that's a part-time salary going on rework you can't see.
  • Half your team may be using AI without telling you. That's not a discipline issue. It usually means there's no policy, so people stay quiet. Hidden use is unchecked use.
  • Polished isn't the same as right. AI output looks finished. That's what makes it expensive: the mistakes surface late, in front of a client or a manager.
  • If you're the one fixing it, that's leverage. The person who can make AI produce work that doesn't need fixing is becoming one of the most valuable people in any office. That's a career skill, not a tech skill.
  • Using AI to fix AI only works with context. Half the fixers in the survey used AI to correct AI. Without your real documents and rules, you just get a second, differently wrong draft.

How do I stop AI slop in my team?

  1. Give the AI your context once, not in every chat. Set up one shared workspace (a ChatGPT or Claude Project, or a Gemini Gem) with your real documents, house rules, a price list, two good examples and the words you never use. Most slop comes from AI guessing what your business is.
  2. Make "AI helped" a normal sentence. Agree a one-line note on anything that leaves someone's desk: "Drafted with AI, numbers checked by me." No blame, no ban. You can't check what you can't see.
  3. The person who sends it checks it. Not the manager, not the next person in line. If you hand it on, you own it. This one rule removes most of the "I'll just redo it" work.
  4. Check facts, not tone. AI is usually good at tone and bad at your facts. Check numbers, names, dates, prices and anything promised to a client. Use the prompt below to make the AI flag what needs checking.
  5. When the same mistake comes back twice, fix the setup. Add the rule or the example to the shared workspace instead of correcting the draft again. That's how an AI tool gets better every week instead of staying average. My piece on measuring AI's value shows how to count the hours this saves.

Copy this prompt: the "before you send it" check

Paste this at the end of any AI draft, in ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. It makes the AI tell you what it guessed, so you know exactly what to check before it goes to a colleague or a client.

Before-you-send-it check 
Before I send this draft to [a colleague / my manager / a client], review it as a strict editor who knows nothing about my business except what I've told you in this conversation and the files I've shared.

1. List every fact in the draft: numbers, names, dates, prices, product details, promises and deadlines. For each one, say where it came from: (a) something I gave you, (b) a file I shared, or (c) your own assumption or general knowledge.
2. Put everything marked (c) at the top under "Check these before sending".
3. Point out anything that sounds confident but is vague, generic or could apply to any company.
4. Flag anything that could embarrass me if it's wrong in front of the reader.
5. Tell me what one piece of context, if I added it to my AI workspace, would have prevented the biggest problem.

Don't rewrite the draft. Don't invent sources. If you're unsure where something came from, mark it (c).

After

Also today in AI

Visa tested 22 AI models as shoppers. They failed 42.8% of the time

On 5 November Visa published what it calls a first-of-its-kind benchmark for AI shopping agents: 22 models from 12 labs, 15 shopping tasks such as buying a TV or a laptop, where the agent has to ask questions to find the buyer's real needs. Models failed 42.8% of runs, the best passed 82.2% and the worst 28.9%, as The Next Web reported from Visa's announcement. The worrying part: in 54% of failed runs the AI still made a purchase. Visa's Rubail Birwadker: "We're not there yet from a trust or technology perspective."

What it means for you: it's the same lesson as the lead story, with money attached. Let AI research and shortlist; keep the final "buy" or "send" click for a human. If you sell online, make your product pages answer the questions an agent would ask (size, compatibility, delivery, returns) in plain text.

DeepSeek's open-source agent app passes 1.54 million daily users

DeepSeek shared usage numbers for DeepSeek Harness, its open-source agent framework, for the first time: 1.54 million daily active users in the week ended 4 November, up from 429,000 in the week ended 7 October, according to Yicai Global. Users have created about 35,000 third-party plug-ins, and around 60% have built or installed at least one. These are DeepSeek's own figures and Yicai doesn't explain how daily users are counted.

What it means for you: agents that run tasks on your computer are going mainstream fast, including outside the big US labs. Before you install any of them, check what it can read and run on your machine, and start it on a folder with nothing sensitive in it.

FAQ

What is AI workslop or AI slop at work?

It's work produced with AI that looks finished but isn't: wrong numbers, generic advice, invented details or a tone that doesn't fit. The cost lands on the person who receives it and has to check, fix or redo it.

How much time do workers spend fixing AI mistakes?

A survey of 2,000 working adults commissioned by Arden University, reported in November 2026, found an average of 6 hours 57 minutes a week correcting colleagues' AI work, with 46% of workers doing it. A Zapier survey reported in January 2026 put revision time at about four and a half hours a week, and Workday found nearly 40% of the time AI saves goes on rework.

Should I ban AI at work to avoid mistakes?

No. The same survey found 53% of employees already don't tell their supervisor they use AI, so a ban mostly pushes it out of sight. A short policy works better: one shared workspace with your context, a one-line note when AI helped, and the rule that whoever sends it checks it.

How do I make AI output more accurate for my business?

Give it your real context once, in a shared Project or Gem: your documents, prices, house rules and two good examples. Then ask it to list every fact it used and where it came from, so you only check the parts it guessed. The prompt in this article does that.

Can I use AI to check AI's work?

Yes, but only with context. Half the people fixing colleagues' AI work in the Arden survey used AI to help. Without your real documents and rules, a second AI pass just gives you a second guess. With them, it's a good first filter before a human check.

Are AI shopping agents safe to use?

Not for buying on your own yet. In Visa's November 2026 benchmark, AI shopping agents failed 42.8% of test runs and still made a purchase in 54% of failed runs. Use them to research and shortlist, and make the final purchase yourself.
