Is your business AI-proof? Check it against 8 moats.

AI is making software, content and answers almost free. Greg Isenberg mapped the eight kinds of business it can't copy overnight. Here's how to score your own business on them, and the five moves I'd make this week.

Short answer

A business is AI-proof when the thing customers pay for can't be copied overnight with a chatbot. Greg Isenberg names eight sources of that protection: physical work (atoms), licenses and partnerships (permission), an audience (attention), being early (timing), community and events (belonging), marketplaces (liquidity), data nobody else has (unique data) and getting paid for results (outcomes). Many small businesses already own some of these without realising it. The job is to find them, write down the ones that live in your head, and use AI on everything else so you have more time to deepen them.

One of my clients runs an events production company in Italy. About 420 events a year. No website. Every client comes by word of mouth.

When we built his first AI agent, it turned a site-visit transcript into three documents: a logistics plan, a technical sheet and the client quote. It worked well until it reached discounts. Then it stopped. His discount policy had never been written down. It lived in his head.

At the time I saw that as a problem to fix. This week I see it differently. That unwritten knowledge is the part of his business no AI can copy. He just didn't know he owned it, because it had never been on paper.

What changed my view was Greg Isenberg's solo episode "8 AI-Proof Businesses I'd build in 2027" on The Startup Ideas Podcast.

What makes a business AI-proof?

Greg's starting point, from the episode description: layoffs keep coming, products get copied pixel for pixel, and AI is pushing the cost of software, content and answers towards zero. He still sees more opportunity than ever, but only in businesses that can't be rebuilt in a weekend.

His clearest example is the first one. A coffee shop. A plumber. About the plumber he says: "AI is not going to be able to fix that leak." He also expects the value of these physical businesses to go up as money from the tech world moves into them.

That's the core idea. If ChatGPT can produce what you sell, your price is heading towards ChatGPT's price. If it can't, AI becomes a cost-cutter for you, not a competitor.

What are the 8 AI-proof moats?

Greg walks through eight categories. I've kept his labels and added what each one looks like in the kind of business I coach. I'm sticking to the parts of the episode I could check; the small-business translation is my own.

  1. Atoms: physical businesses. Coffee shops, plumbers. In my world: contractors, events, clinics, anything where someone has to show up.
  2. Permission: licenses, regulation and partnerships. Firms that need a license or a qualified owner, like law or bookkeeping in many countries, and preferred-vendor status with a big company. Greg suggests partnering with a licensed professional and running the marketing, growth and AI side yourself.
  3. Attention: a media machine. Newsletters, meme pages, founder-led content. Greg calls these "somewhat" AI-proof and says he now uses an AI model as his short-form video editor. Attention is built by a person; AI just makes it cheaper to produce.
  4. Timing: spotting trends early. Being first to a new need, before everyone else notices it.
  5. Belonging: paid communities and IRL events. People pay to be in the room with other people. A chatbot can't be the room.
  6. Liquidity: marketplaces. Connecting buyers and sellers. Once both sides are there, they're hard to move.
  7. Unique data: data brokers and curated directories. Information nobody else has collected and kept clean.
  8. Outcomes: customers paying for results. You charge for the result, not the hours or the software.

You don't need all eight. You need to know which ones you already have, because those are where your time and money should go.

How do I know if my business is AI-proof?

Go back to my events client and score him against the list.

  • Atoms: strong. Stages, crews and venues. Nobody downloads an event.
  • Belonging: strong. 100% of his work comes by word of mouth. That's trust built one event at a time, and it can't be generated.
  • Unique data: strong, but invisible. His pricing logic, his discount rules, what each venue needs. All real, none of it written down.

Three moats. And where did AI fit? Not in the event itself. In the paperwork around it: the quote, the logistics plan, the technical sheet. That's the "free" part of his business, the part AI is good at. Once the agent handles it, he spends more of his week on the parts nobody can copy.

The surprise was the third moat. AI didn't threaten his unique data. It exposed it. To get the agent to apply discounts, he had to write his rules down. That's the moment his knowledge stopped being a risk (it walks out the door with him) and started being an asset.

A second example. An accountant I coach in Italy is building his own audit software with Claude. Accounting is a licensed profession, so he already has permission. Now he's turning his own way of auditing into a tool. The license keeps competitors out; the tool makes his time go further.

And my own business. Marketplaces don't let me share links in messages, so my website and blog had to become my growth channel. I publish a daily AI-news post built with an AI pipeline. That's an attention moat: AI does the heavy lifting, but the point of view and the client stories are mine.

What's the difference between "free" work and moat work?

Here's the framework I now use with clients. I call it the moat audit, and it splits every business into two piles.

Free work is anything a competitor with ChatGPT could produce this weekend: quotes, reports, emails, proposals, social posts, summaries. Valuable, but not protected. Give as much of it as you can to AI.

Moat work is what customers pay you for that can't be produced that way: the job on site, the license, the relationships, the community, the data you've collected over years. Protect it, write it down, and put the time AI saves you back into it.

The mistake I see most often is the reverse. Owners use AI to make their marketing look like everyone else's, while the knowledge that makes them different stays in their head, unwritten and at risk.

How to make your business more AI-proof: 5 moves for this week

  1. Score your 8 moats. 0 for none, 1 for some, 2 for strong. Be honest. Prompt 1 below runs the interview for you.
  2. Run the weekend copy test. Ask: what could a competitor with ChatGPT copy from my business by Monday? Your website copy, your price list, your brochure. That's not your moat, so stop over-investing in it. Prompt 2 plays the competitor.
  3. Write down one rule that lives in your head. A discount rule, how you price a rush job, which supplier you call first and why. One per week. This is the work that turns unique data from a risk into an asset, and it's the same work that makes your AI assistant useful.
  4. Hand one piece of free work to AI. The report you hate writing, the quote you rebuild every time, the weekly email. Pick one task and finish it end to end before you start a second.
  5. Start one data habit. Every job, every quote, every enquiry logged in one sheet with the same columns. In a year that sheet is something no competitor has, and it's what every AI tool you add will learn from.

Prompt 1: the moat audit

10-minute moat score 
You are a blunt business advisor. Help me find out which parts of my business AI can't copy.

Interview me one question at a time (max 10 questions) about what I sell, who buys it, why they choose me, and what I know that competitors don't.

Then score my business 0, 1 or 2 on each of these 8 moats, with one sentence of evidence from my answers:
atoms (physical work), permission (licenses, regulation, partnerships), attention (an audience I own), timing (being early), belonging (community, events, word of mouth), liquidity (connecting buyers and sellers), unique data (records, rules or know-how nobody else has), outcomes (getting paid for results).

Finally:
1. List my top 3 moats.
2. List the knowledge behind them that is NOT written down anywhere.
3. List 5 tasks in my week that are "free work" an AI could do, ranked by hours saved.

Prompt 2: the weekend copy test

Play your own competitor 
Act as a new competitor who wants to steal my customers. You have one weekend, ChatGPT and a small budget.

Here is what my business does and how it shows up online: [paste your website text, price list or a description of your business].

1. List everything you could copy or match by Monday (copy, offers, prices, content, the look of my brand).
2. List what you could NOT copy, even with a year and real money, and explain why.
3. Tell me where I'm spending time or money on the things in list 1 instead of the things in list 2.
Be specific and don't flatter me.

Is this an opportunity for AI consultants and freelancers?

Yes, and one idea in the episode is aimed squarely at you. In the permission section, Greg suggests partnering with a licensed professional, such as a lawyer, and handling the marketing, growth and AI side yourself. The license is their moat. Your skills make it pay.

My own take for consultants: the moat audit is a sellable first session. Run the two prompts with an owner, write down the three rules that live in their head, and automate one piece of free work. It's concrete, it's done in a week, and it naturally leads to the bigger job of building their AI assistant on top of those written rules. FAQ

What is an AI-proof business?

A business whose core value can't be copied quickly with AI tools: physical work, licenses, an owned audience, community, marketplaces, unique data or paid-for results. AI can still help run it, but it can't replace what customers pay for.

What are Greg Isenberg's 8 AI-proof businesses?

In his November 2026 episode of The Startup Ideas Podcast he groups them as atoms (physical businesses), permission (licenses, regulation, partnerships), attention (media), timing (spotting trends early), belonging (paid communities and IRL events), liquidity (marketplaces), unique data (data brokers and curated directories) and outcomes (customers paying for results).

Is a small local business safe from AI?

Usually safer than an online-only one, because physical work, local trust and word of mouth are hard to copy. The weak spot is the knowledge that lives only in the owner's head. Write it down so it becomes an asset instead of a risk.

How do I check if my business is AI-proof?

Score it 0 to 2 on each of the 8 moats, then ask an AI to act as a competitor with one weekend and ChatGPT and list what it could copy by Monday. What it can't copy is your moat.

Should I still use AI if my business is AI-proof?

Yes. Use AI on the "free work" around your moat, such as quotes, reports and emails, so you spend more time on what customers actually pay you for.

How can AI consultants use the 8 moats?

As a first paid session: score a client's moats, document the rules behind them, and automate one piece of routine work. Greg Isenberg also suggests partnering with licensed professionals and running the marketing, growth and AI side for them.
