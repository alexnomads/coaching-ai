2 million planted secrets show where your AI prompts really go

A security researcher hid nearly two million fake passwords and keys inside AI prompts and watched where they turned up. Some were stolen from breached AI hosts, some were read days later, one surfaced in a company's internal chat 24 days on. Here's what happened, what it means if you use AI at work, and the six habits I'd adopt this week.

Short answer

HD Moore, chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, sent about two million AI prompts containing unique fake secrets (unused cloud keys, passwords, private links) and tracked where they went. He found evidence that attackers had breached three AI hosting providers, saw around 200 of the planted cloud keys exposed in late October, and found prompts routed through resellers and undisclosed marketplaces, some reaching Chinese AI companies. "Zero data retention" labels didn't stop it. If you use AI at work: never paste live passwords or keys, prefer tools that talk to the model maker directly, and ask every AI vendor who actually runs the model.

When clients ask me "is it safe to paste this into AI?", they usually picture one company: OpenAI, Google, Anthropic. This week's research shows that's often not the picture. Between you and the model there can be an app, a router, a marketplace and a reseller, and each one is a place your words can be logged, read or stolen.

Most of the people I coach will never touch an API. But plenty of the cheap "all models in one app" tools, browser extensions and plugins they use do. So this one is worth ten minutes.

What did the AI prompt leak research find?

Dragos published the research on 19 November, and CyberSec Media summarised it the next day. AI Weekly added details from Ars Technica's write-up. The method is simple: each prompt carried a "canary", a fake secret used nowhere else and linked to a server the researcher controlled. If anyone ever used it, he'd know which prompt, and which provider, it came from.

The line that stayed with me, from CyberSec Media's summary: "Some sellers could access and retain prompts simply by enabling standard request logging". No hacking needed. Just a setting.

Does this mean ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini leak my data?

That's not what the research says. The problems sat in the chain of hosts, resellers and marketplaces that serve models on behalf of others, and the breached hosts weren't named. If you type into the official ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini app, your prompt goes to the company that made the model, under that company's terms.

The risk grows with every hop you can't see: the kind of cheap app that offers dozens of models for one small subscription, the Chrome extension that "rewrites any email", the automation that calls whichever model is cheapest today. You may never know which company actually ran your prompt, and its privacy promise may not bind the reseller behind it. Dragos's own advice is to treat retention promises as requirements to test, not guarantees.

The model name on the button tells you what answered. It doesn't tell you who read the question.

Why it matters to you

  • People paste secrets into AI every day. Passwords in a "fix this config" request, API keys in a script, client names and bank details in a "summarise this email". Those are exactly the things this research planted, and exactly what got used.
  • "Zero data retention" is a label, not a lock. Some compromised requests were marked zero data retention. The label didn't help against attackers who were already inside a host.
  • Leaks are slow and quiet. One planted link resurfaced after 24 days. You wouldn't connect the two events.
  • It's the same lesson as the Notion leak. Last week I wrote about Notion's AI chats leaking through a vendor. The weakest link is rarely the brand you chose. It's the company behind the company.

How do I keep my data safe when using AI tools?

  1. Never paste a live password, API key or card number. Replace it with [PASSWORD] or [KEY]. The AI doesn't need the real value to help you.
  2. If you already did, rotate it. Change any password or key you've pasted into an AI tool you're not sure about. It takes five minutes and closes the door this research found open.
  3. Prefer the direct route for sensitive work. Use the official ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini app, or your company's approved business plan, for client and company data. Keep the multi-model apps for low-stakes tasks.
  4. List your AI tools. Apps, extensions, plugins, automations. For each, write down which model it uses and whether it says who hosts it. Anything you can't answer goes on a "no client data" list.
  5. Ask vendors one question in writing. "Which companies process my prompts, and do your data retention terms bind all of them?" Keep the answer. The prompt below drafts it for you.
  6. Strip names before you paste. Swap client names, emails and amounts for placeholders when the task doesn't need them. "Client A owes €X" gets the same email draft.

If you use AI agents with access to your accounts, the stakes are higher: my post on AI agents breaking in with old passwords covers the password side.

Copy this prompt: audit your AI tools in ten minutes

Paste this into the official ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini app (not a third-party tool), then fill in your list.

AI tool supply-chain check 
You are helping a non-technical professional check the privacy of the AI tools they use for work. Do not guess: if something isn't known, write "ASK VENDOR".

Here are the AI tools I use (apps, browser extensions, plugins, automations), what I use each for, and what kind of data I put in:
[TOOL 1] - [what I use it for] - [data: e.g. client emails, contracts, passwords, nothing sensitive]
[TOOL 2] - ...
[TOOL 3] - ...

For each tool, give me a table with:
1. Who makes the AI model it uses (if known).
2. Whether it likely sends my prompts to the model maker directly or through a router, marketplace or reseller.
3. A risk level (low / medium / high) based on the data I put in.
4. One concrete change, e.g. "remove client names", "use the official app instead", "fine as is".

Then:
- List every tool where I should stop pasting client or company data until the vendor answers.
- Write a short, polite email I can send to those vendors asking: which companies process my prompts, whether prompts are logged or used for training, and whether their data retention promises bind every company in that chain.
- Remind me which passwords or keys I should change if I've ever pasted them into a "high" tool.
Before

Paste the config file with the real password into whichever AI tool is open, because the button says "GPT" or "Claude" so it must be fine.

After

Placeholders instead of secrets, client work only in the model maker's own app, and a written answer from every vendor about who sees your prompts.

FAQ

What did the Dragos AI prompt research find?

HD Moore of Dragos sent about two million AI prompts containing unique fake secrets and tracked their use. He found evidence that attackers had breached three AI hosting providers, around 200 planted AWS keys were exposed in late October, and some prompts were routed through undisclosed marketplaces or reached Chinese AI organisations.

Is it safe to paste passwords or API keys into ChatGPT or other AI tools?

No. Replace them with placeholders like [PASSWORD]. The AI doesn't need the real value to help, and this research shows secrets in prompts can be logged or stolen along the way. If you've already pasted one into a tool you're not sure about, change it.

Does "zero data retention" mean my prompts are never stored?

Not reliably. Some compromised requests in the research were marked zero data retention, and the label didn't protect them from attackers already inside a host. Dragos advises treating retention promises as requirements to test, not guarantees.

Were ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini breached?

The research doesn't say so. The breached AI hosting providers weren't named, and the problems were in the chain of hosts, resellers and marketplaces that serve models for others. Using a model maker's own app keeps that chain short.

What is an AI router or reseller?

A service that sits between an app and the AI model, choosing which company runs the model for each request, often to cut cost. Many multi-model apps and extensions rely on them, and each adds a company that can see your prompt.

How can I check which AI tools are safe for client data?

List every AI app, extension and automation you use, note which model each uses and who hosts it, and ask vendors in writing which companies process your prompts. Keep client data in tools that can answer clearly.
