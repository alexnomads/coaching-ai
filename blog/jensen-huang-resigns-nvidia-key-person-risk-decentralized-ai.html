Jensen Huang's exit and the $4 trillion single point of failure

Nvidia's CEO resigned, one man's absence shook the world's AI chipmaker, and 150,000 people signed up to bring him back. Here's what happened this week, what crypto already taught us about key-person risk, and what decentralized AI projects like Akash and Venice are trying to fix.

Short answer

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO on 12 November 2026 after reports of three extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected of spying for China. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. The stock fell sharply, a major investor stopped buying, and a petition to reinstate him passed 150,000 signatures. Nothing about Nvidia's products changed, which is the point: the market priced a person. For anyone building on AI, the lesson is key-person and single-provider risk, and it's the problem decentralized projects such as Akash Network (computing power) and Venice (private AI) are built to reduce.

If crypto taught us one thing, it's this: don't build critical infrastructure around a single point of failure.

Last week the AI economy found out it had one.

Why did Jensen Huang resign as Nvidia CEO?

Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and ran it for 33 years, resigned following reports that he had multiple extramarital relationships, including one with a suspected agent of China and another with an executive who reported to him. Before he left, Nvidia's board had opened an independent investigation into reporting that he had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Nvidia did not give a reason for the departure.

In its filing announcing the CEO transition, Nvidia named CFO Colette Kress interim CEO and Harvey C. Jones its first-ever board chair.

The week in six points

  1. Huang resigned on 12 November, effective immediately, from the CEO role and the board.
  2. An investor stopped buying. Billionaire Leo KoGuan, who says he has put about $1.3 billion into Nvidia, told Bloomberg: "No further investment without Jensen."
  3. Elon Musk said hiring Huang "would be an honor", replying on X to a post that read "Elon please hire Jensen", the night before the resignation. No offer has been reported.
  4. A "Stand with Jensen" petition passed 150,000 signatures, backed by Musk, Epic Games' Tim Sweeney and other tech leaders. The campaign site lists thousands of signers as Nvidia shareholders and employees.
  5. Two major shareholders wrote to the board. Altimeter's Brad Gerstner and Atreides' Gavin Baker asked for a slow CEO search that keeps Huang's return open, stopping short of demanding immediate reinstatement.
  6. Huang is discussing a comeback. He has talked to allies about returning, the Financial Times reported, while supporters organise a "war room" to lobby the board. Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday.

Why did Nvidia stock fall when Huang left?

Nvidia's chips didn't get worse last Thursday. The products, the roadmap and the customers were the same as the day before. Yet the shares fell more than 8% in extended trading on the news, and the saga drove the stock to eight-month lows.

That's key-person risk: when a company's value depends heavily on one individual, losing that individual is priced as if the business changed. Two things are true at once:

  • The governance worked. Huang did not have a controlling stake that would have let him overturn the board, so a group of directors could act.
  • The market still treated one person as the asset. A company worth around $4 trillion moved hard on a single name, and shareholders are petitioning for a person rather than a strategy.
Leaders leave. Networks, and businesses, shouldn't break when they do.

What should crypto have taught us about centralization?

Bitcoin's creator disappeared years ago, and the network kept running. Crypto also learned the opposite lesson the hard way: FTX and Terra collapsed because enormous trust sat with a few people.

Decentralization doesn't mean having no leaders. It means no single person whose absence, scandal or bad decision can break the system.

AI today is concentrated at every layer. Computing power sits with a handful of chip makers and cloud providers. The leading models come from a few labs. And a few companies decide what you can ask, what gets logged and what it costs. When that much sits with so few people, their scandals, policy changes and outages become your risk too.

What is Akash Network (AKT)?

Akash Network is an open marketplace where anyone can rent GPU and cloud computing from independent providers, instead of from a single cloud company. Its products include the Akash Marketplace, the AkashML inference platform and Akash Homenode, which lets people contribute their own computing power.

Last week Akash moved its token to the Solana blockchain. 97.3% of participating AKT holders voted for the move away from its own Cosmos-based chain, and AKT went live on Solana on 12 November, with a one-way 1:1 migration portal open for two years. Moving the computing marketplace itself still needs a separate vote.

Why it matters: GPU supply from many providers, priced in an open market, with no single company able to switch you off. And by leaving its own chain, Akash stops paying to secure infrastructure it doesn't need.

What is Venice AI and the VVV token?

Venice is a private AI platform founded by Erik Voorhees, the founder of ShapeShift. It says it keeps no logs and requires no account, and it runs open-weight models on its own infrastructure. Instead of paying a monthly subscription, users can stake VVV for a permanent share of the platform's AI capacity.

Why it matters: privacy by design. Your prompts don't become someone else's profile of you, and access doesn't depend on one company's subscription rules.

Is decentralized AI risk-free?

No. Less key-person risk is not the same as no risk:

  • Akash's core team still steers big decisions; they chose the move to Solana.
  • Venice is still a company with a founder.
  • These tokens are extremely volatile. VVV went from $0.92 to a record $34 within a year, and what goes up that fast can come down just as fast.

What does this mean for a business using AI?

You probably don't run a $4 trillion company. But if you use AI at work, you have the same exposure on a smaller scale. Most businesses I coach have built their AI workflows on one provider, one model and one account. Here's the check I run with them:

  1. List your dependencies. Which AI tools does your business rely on every day, and what happens if one goes down, changes its price or changes its rules?
  2. Keep your know-how portable. Store your instructions, prompts and examples in your own files, not only inside one app, so you can move them to another model in an afternoon. Open formats like Agent Skills make that easier.
  3. Test a second model. Run your most important task on a different provider once a month, so switching is a decision, not a crisis.
  4. Decide what must stay private. For client data and sensitive work, check where your prompts are stored and who can read them.
  5. Separate the leader from the system. If your AI setup only works because one person understands it, document it.
