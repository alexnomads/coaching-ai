Let AI audit your business. Then make it show its evidence.

The newest AI models can work through your website, prices, emails and competitors and hand you a ranked list of money moves. That list is only worth something if every line points back to a real file. Here's the four-step audit I run with business owners, five moves for this week, and two prompts to copy.

Short answer

You can ask an AI model to audit your business and rank the ten changes most likely to make you money. The audit is only useful if you feed it real files and make it cite evidence for every finding. Run it in four steps: Feed (your website, prices, quotes, unpaid invoices, enquiries and three competitors), Rank (ten moves, not forty ideas), Prove (each item names the file and line behind it, plus a list of data that's missing) and Fix (one change this week, and write down the rule it exposed). In my experience the most valuable finding is often a gap in your own data, not a clever idea.

The first time we tested his new AI setup on his accounts, it found money.

My client runs an electrical contracting business in Sydney. Eight technicians, office support offshore, and about 70% of his enquiries arrive on WhatsApp. We had spent the session connecting an AI workspace to his job-management and accounting systems. On the very first test of the accounts chat, it surfaced an overdue payment that nobody in the business had flagged.

Then we ran a lead report. It found a lead that nobody had worked.

Good start. But I was more interested in what happened next. When we tried to build a proper report by type of client, it fell apart. Most of his jobs had no client category at all. The AI couldn't sort what had never been labelled. So before any dashboard, we wrote a categorisation rulebook: plain rules for which category each job belongs to. A report that used to take him about five minutes now takes about one. But the rulebook was the real result.

I thought about that session this week, because the most useful idea I heard about AI models wasn't about a feature. It was about an audit.

What is an AI business audit?

In The Startup Ideas Podcast episode on Claude Fable 5.2 (11 Nov 2026), Greg Isenberg walks through twelve prompts for the new model. The first one is the one I'd start any business owner with. According to Profitable Founder's write-up of the episode, it opens: "Audit my entire business. The website, pricing, onboarding, emails, and top 5 competitors."

The model is asked to keep working until it's done, keep a running log, and return a ranked list of the ten changes most likely to make money, with evidence for each one. The write-up says Greg ran it on his design agency and runs it on every business his studio incubates. His wider point is that models like this can now work on a job for many hours without hand-holding, so you can hand them a whole problem, not one question at a time.

That's his idea, and it's a good one. What follows is mine: how I'd run that audit for a small business that doesn't have a studio's tidy data, and what to do with the answer.

Why do most AI audits give generic advice?

Because they're fed a description, not the business.

If you type "I run a plumbing company with 6 vans, how can I make more money?", you'll get a well-written list that could apply to any plumbing company on earth. Follow up faster. Ask for reviews. Raise prices. None of it is wrong. None of it is yours.

The words that matter in Greg's prompt aren't "audit my business". They're "with evidence". An audit that can't point at your quotes, your invoices and your enquiries is an opinion. And an AI opinion is fluent enough that it's easy to mistake for a finding.

A finding without a source is a guess. Make the AI show you the file and the line, or take it off the list.

How do you run an evidence-first AI audit? Feed, Rank, Prove, Fix

1. Feed. Give it the real material. Your website link, your price list, your last 20 quotes, the list of unpaid invoices, your last 30 enquiries (an export of the WhatsApp chat or inbox is fine), and the websites of three competitors. Put it all in a project or workspace with memory, not a fresh chat, so you can come back to it.

2. Rank. Ask for ten changes, ranked by how likely they are to make or save money in the next 90 days. Ten is a deliberate limit. Forty ideas is a to-do list you'll never open again.

3. Prove. This is the step people skip. Every item must name the file and the line, row or page it's based on. Then ask for a second list: data the AI needed but couldn't find. In the Sydney business, that second list would have been the most valuable page in the audit.

4. Fix. Pick one item. Fix it this week. Then write down the rule the audit exposed (who chases unpaid invoices and when, how a job gets categorised, what discount you're allowed to give) and add it to the project. Next month, run the audit again on better data.

What does the evidence look like in practice?

Look at what the Sydney tests actually found. An overdue payment: you can open the invoice. A lead nobody worked: you can open the message. Those are findings. Nobody had to trust the AI, because the evidence was one click away.

Compare that with the kind of advice a description-only audit produces:

The guess and the evidence might point to the same action. The difference is that you can check the evidence in two minutes, and you can't check the guess at all. With a model that writes this well, that's the whole game.

And when the AI says "I couldn't find this", that isn't a failure. It's the audit doing its job. I've seen the same pattern with an events company in Italy, where an agent couldn't apply a discount because the discount policy had never been written down. AI keeps asking businesses for rules that only exist in the owner's head.

How do you do it this week? 5 moves

  1. Build the audit pack (one hour). One folder: website link, price list, last 20 quotes, unpaid invoices, last 30 enquiries, three competitor links. Exports and screenshots of tables are fine. Before you upload customer data, check your AI plan's data settings, and remove anything you wouldn't want stored.
  2. Run the audit in a project. Use the first prompt below. Give it time. If your tool supports longer tasks, let it work through every file rather than skimming.
  3. Strike every unsourced line. Anything without a file and a line comes off. Then open the source for the top three yourself. If one doesn't hold up, the whole list drops a level of trust.
  4. Red-team the top move. Use the second prompt to make the AI argue against its own first choice. This is borrowed from another of Greg's prompts in the same episode, which asks the model to argue against your plan like a sceptical investor.
  5. Fix one thing, write one rule. Make the change. Then write the missing rule into your project instructions so the next audit, and every other AI task, starts from it.
Prompt 1 · The evidence-first audit 
You are auditing my business, [business name], which does [what] for [who] in [city/region].

Attached: my website link, price list, last 20 quotes, unpaid invoices, last 30 enquiries, and 3 competitor websites.

Work through every file before you answer. Keep a short log of what you read.

Then give me:
1. The 10 changes most likely to make or save money in the next 90 days, ranked.
   For each: what to change, why, and the evidence (file name + the exact line, row or page).
   If you can't point to evidence in my files, don't include it.
2. A list of data you needed but couldn't find, and what each gap stops you from seeing.
3. The one change you'd make this week, and the first step.

Don't give general advice that would apply to any business like mine.
Prompt 2 · Red-team the top move 
Take the number 1 change from your audit.

Argue against it as hard as you can, like a sceptical business partner who has to approve the time and money.
- What could go wrong, and what would it cost?
- What in my files contradicts it, or is too thin to support it?
- What would a smaller, cheaper test of the same idea look like?

Finish with one sentence: what would change your mind and make you back it?

What should you do with the "missing data" list?

Treat it as the start of your rulebook. In the Sydney business, the missing client categories became a written list of rules. Once that existed, the AI could sort jobs, the reports made sense, and the owner, who works from his phone and thinks visually, could finally get the mobile dashboard he actually wanted instead of answers in a chat.

I've seen a version of this in a Dubai real-estate agency too: the plan was a campaign to thousands of existing contacts, and it paused until the CRM was cleaned. Clean data comes before clever campaigns. An audit that tells you where your data is dirty has saved you from building on it.

So when the gap list is long, don't be embarrassed. Pick the gap that blocks the most money and fix that first. Usually it's one of three: who owes you, where leads come from, or what kind of job each one is.

Is this an opportunity for AI consultants and freelancers?

Yes. In the same episode, Greg's argument, as the write-up summarises it, is to stop billing hours and sell the finished result at a fixed price. An evidence-first audit is a natural fixed-price product: a ranked list, a gap list, and one fix done with the client.

My advice if you're building that offer: sell the evidence, not the AI. Clients don't care how many hours the model ran. They care that item 3 points at an invoice they can open. Make the gap list part of the deliverable, because it leads straight to the next piece of work: writing the rules, cleaning the data, then building the dashboard or agent on top. And keep the client's hands on it. The goal is that they can run the audit again next quarter without you. That's exactly what a 49€ diagnostic is, in a small version.

FAQ

What is an AI business audit?

An AI business audit is when you give an AI model your real business material (website, prices, quotes, invoices, enquiries, competitors) and ask it to rank the changes most likely to make or save money, with evidence for each finding.

What should I give the AI for a business audit?

Your website link, price list, recent quotes, unpaid invoices, recent customer enquiries and three competitor websites. Real files beat a written description, because the AI can only cite evidence it can see.

How do I know the AI's audit is right?

Make it cite the file and the exact line, row or page behind every finding, remove anything without a source, and open the sources for the top three items yourself before acting.

Which AI model should I use for a business audit?

Any current model that can read your files and work in a project with memory will do. Newer models can work on long tasks with less supervision, which helps when the audit pack is large. The evidence rule matters more than the model.

Is it safe to upload business data to an AI tool?

Check your plan's data and training settings first, use a business or team plan where possible, and remove personal customer details you don't need for the audit.

Can consultants sell AI audits as a service?

Yes. A fixed-price audit that delivers a ranked, evidence-backed list, a list of data gaps and one completed fix is easy for a small business to understand, and the gap list leads naturally to follow-on work.
