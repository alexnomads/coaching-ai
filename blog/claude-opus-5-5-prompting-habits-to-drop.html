Stop telling Claude to think hard. Seven prompting habits to unlearn.

Anthropic says some of the prompting tricks we all learned are now slowing Claude Opus 5.5 down. Here's a lesson on what to drop, what to do instead, and a copy-paste prompt that updates your old saved prompts for you.

Short answer

With Claude Opus 5.5, stop adding "think hard" or "think carefully" to your prompts, start on Medium effort instead of the highest setting, and replace long step-by-step chats with one clear brief. Anthropic's guidance says the model already decides how much to think, and that removing those phrases makes replies start sooner with no clear drop in quality. It also recommends attaching charts and files instead of typing them out, asking for the finished document straight away, connecting your files and tools, and naming exactly what you don't want instead of saying "don't make it generic".

I read Anthropic's new prompting guide for Claude Opus 5.5 this morning, and one line stopped me. In its list of design clichés to ban by name, Anthropic includes cream backgrounds and italic headline accents.

That's my website. You're looking at it.

It's a good reminder of the point of the whole guide: the model has changed, and some of what worked a year ago now gets in the way. Many of the prompts I see from new students were written for older models. They're full of "think step by step", "be thorough", ten follow-up messages and a request for a draft "so I can check it first". One of my clients once spent around 11 hours and more than 200 prompts trying to get a single safety form right. The answer wasn't a cleverer prompt. It was less of the old ritual and more of the right context.

What did Anthropic say about prompting Claude Opus 5.5?

Anthropic published a prompting guide for Opus 5.5 in its developer docs, plus a guide for everyday knowledge work in the Claude app, summarised by The Decoder on 14 November. The headline: Opus 5.5 always reasons and adjusts how deeply it thinks to the task, so many old "make it try harder" tricks just add delay.

A few facts that matter for everyday users:

  • Opus 5.5 is the default model on paid Claude plans, according to The Decoder.
  • Its default effort level is Medium. Anthropic's docs say Medium on Opus 5.5 matches or beats Opus 5 on High in its coding and knowledge-work tests.
  • The docs say it generates text more than 30% faster than Opus 5 and usually uses fewer tokens for the same task.
  • Anthropic says it's much less likely to state a wrong figure or cite the wrong source, and that documents, spreadsheets and slides need less editing.

Which prompting habits should I drop with Claude Opus 5.5?

1. "Think hard" and "think carefully"

This was a genuine trick with older models. Anthropic now says to remove these lines and let the model set its own thinking depth. In its tests, replies started sooner with no clear decline in quality. If you've pasted "think step by step" into your Project instructions or custom instructions, this is the first thing to delete.

2. Maxing out the effort setting

More effort means more thinking, more waiting and more of your usage. The Decoder sums up Anthropic's advice in one line: "Higher settings cost more and don't always produce better results." Start on Medium. Move up to High only when you see it fall short on a specific task, like a complex financial model or a long legal comparison. In the Claude app the effort choice sits in the model menu next to the send button, according to The Decoder.

3. Chatting your way there, step by step

Many people "warm up" the AI: first message about the business, second about the task, third about the format, then corrections. Anthropic's knowledge-work guide recommends describing the result you want in one message: the goal, the output format, and when Claude should stop and ask you questions. That last part is the one almost nobody writes.

4. Typing out charts and tables

If you've been copying numbers from a chart into the chat, stop. Anthropic says Opus 5.5 reads dense charts, diagrams and screenshots more accurately, and that even at low effort it read chart values more accurately than Opus 5 at high. Attach the screenshot, the PDF or the spreadsheet.

5. Asking for a draft first

"Give me a rough draft and then we'll do the real one" doubles the work. Ask for the finished document, spreadsheet or deck directly, then correct what's wrong. You'll often find there's less to fix than you expected.

6. Explaining your business from scratch every time

Anthropic recommends connecting your files and systems so Claude can work from your real material. In practice: a Project with your documents and house rules, and connectors to the tools you already use (email, calendar, drive). This is the single biggest change I make with clients, and it's why I call AI a new employee rather than a tool: you onboard it once.

7. Vague style bans

"Don't make it look generic" or "avoid AI-sounding writing" doesn't work, Anthropic says, because the model just swaps one default for another. Name the specific things you don't want. For writing, that might be "no rhetorical questions, no 'game-changer', no bullet points in the email". For design, Anthropic's example list includes cream backgrounds and italic headline accents. (I'm keeping mine. I chose them on purpose.)

Old prompting was about pushing the model to try harder. New prompting is about telling it clearly what done looks like.

Is there anything else Anthropic says to stop asking for?

  • Don't ask it to show all its internal reasoning in the answer. Anthropic's docs say requests to reproduce its internal reasoning may be declined. Ask for a short explanation of how it reached the answer instead.
  • In a Q&A setup, let answers stay answered. For chat assistants, Anthropic suggests the line "Once you have answered something, treat that answer as done." It reduces second-guessing, but Anthropic warns it can also make the model less likely to flag its own earlier mistakes, so I'd only use it for simple question-and-answer bots.
  • Don't assume a bigger model is always pricier per job. The Decoder notes that Sonnet pushed to its highest effort levels costs about as much as Opus 5.5, and a more capable model can finish in fewer steps. I made the same point about cost per job, not per word.

How do I write one good brief instead of ten messages?

This is the five-part brief I teach. It fits in one message and works in Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini.

  1. Goal. What you want done and who it's for. "A one-page summary of last month's sales for my business partner, who reads it on her phone."
  2. Context. Attach the spreadsheet, the screenshot, last month's version. Don't describe what you can attach.
  3. Output. The finished format. "A Word document with a title, three headline numbers and five bullet points."
  4. Rules. What to avoid, named specifically. "No jargon, no tables wider than three columns, euros not dollars."
  5. Stop. When to pause. "If a number looks wrong or a month is missing, stop and ask me before writing."
Before

"You are a world-class analyst. Think step by step and be very thorough. IMPORTANT: be accurate." Then six follow-ups, a pasted table, and "OK, now the final version."

After

One message: the goal, the attached spreadsheet, the finished format, three named rules and when to stop and ask. Medium effort. The first answer is the one you use.

Copy this prompt: modernise your old saved prompts

Most people have a few prompts they reuse, or Project instructions they wrote a year ago. Paste one into Claude with this and let it clean it up.

Prompt moderniser for Claude Opus 5.5 
Here is a prompt I reuse. Rewrite it for a modern reasoning model.

My old prompt:
[paste your prompt or Project instructions here]

Rules for the rewrite:
1. Remove instructions about how hard to think ("think step by step", "think carefully", "be very thorough").
2. Remove role-play and pressure language that adds nothing ("world-class expert", "IMPORTANT", "you will be penalised").
3. Make sure it states: the goal and who it's for, what context I'll attach, the exact finished output format, specific things to avoid, and when to stop and ask me.
4. Replace vague bans ("don't sound generic") with named, specific ones. If you need examples from me, list the questions.
5. Ask for the finished output directly, not a draft.
6. Keep everything that is specific to my business.

Show me: the new prompt, then a short list of what you removed and why.

Do these tips apply to ChatGPT and Gemini too?

Anthropic's guide is about Claude Opus 5.5, so I'd treat it as Claude-specific. That said, the brief-not-chat habit, attaching files instead of retyping them and naming exactly what you don't want are good practice with any modern assistant in my experience. The effort settings differ by product, so check each app's own model menu.

FAQ

Should I still write "think step by step" in Claude prompts?

Not with Claude Opus 5.5. Anthropic says the model always reasons and adjusts its thinking depth to the task, and that removing "think carefully" style instructions made replies start sooner with no clear decline in quality.

Which effort level should I use with Claude Opus 5.5?

Start on Medium, which is the default. Anthropic's docs say Opus 5.5 on Medium matches or beats Opus 5 on High in its coding and knowledge-work tests. Move to High only when Medium falls short on a specific task.

Is it better to chat step by step or write one long prompt?

Anthropic recommends describing the result you want in one message: the goal, the output format, and when Claude should stop and ask questions. One clear brief usually beats a long back-and-forth.

Can Claude read charts and screenshots accurately?

Anthropic says Opus 5.5 reads dense charts, diagrams and screenshots more accurately than Opus 5, even at low effort. Attach the image or file instead of typing the numbers out.

How do I stop Claude's output looking generic?

Name the specific things you don't want. Anthropic says vague bans like "avoid a generic look" just swap one default for another, while a named list of patterns to avoid works better. The same applies to writing style.

Can I ask Claude to show its full reasoning?

Anthropic's docs say requests to reproduce its internal reasoning in the reply may be declined. Ask for a short explanation of how it reached the answer instead.
