Don't send a proposal. Send the demo.

The fastest way to win an AI client, or to hire one, is a small working thing built from the buyer's own material. Here's the idea I took from Greg Isenberg this week, a story from a client in Italy, and five moves you can make before Friday.

Short answer

A proposal asks a buyer to imagine your work. A demo lets them see it. Greg Isenberg's idea: take a prospect's public website, have AI build a useful little tool or page from it, and send that instead of a pitch. I'd add three rules: use their material, solve one job, and ask for one small next step. It works for AI consultants selling services and for business owners deciding who to hire. Start this week with one prospect and one demo.

I coach the marketing director of a natural-paving manufacturer in Italy. They run marketing and export alone. Their company was main sponsor of a design symposium in Milan, and they needed a video for it.

The usual route to a yes is a proposal: a deck about what AI video could do, why it's safe, what it might cost. Then you hope the CEO has time to read it.

We made the video instead, a split-screen piece, in our lessons. The CEO watched nine seconds and approved it.

Nine seconds. Nobody had to imagine anything. They could see it.

That story came back to me when I listened to Greg Isenberg's short solo episode on Google's Gemini 4 Neon model, because buried in the "ways to make money" section is the same move, aimed at a different buyer.

What did Greg Isenberg say about making money with Gemini 4 Neon?

The episode is "Gemini 4 Neon: the most underrated model of the year", on The Startup Ideas Podcast, published on 20 November 2026. It runs about 16 minutes. Greg tested the model inside Google's Antigravity and says it's good and cheap. The transcript also says Google sponsored the episode, so weigh his enthusiasm with that in mind. I'm not here to review the model. I'm interested in one idea he lists among his ways to make money with it.

Going by the auto-generated transcript, he names five: playable lead magnets such as 3D configurators and mini-games that capture emails; custom demos you build for a company and send as a pitch; live margin and pricing simulators built from messy invoices; back-office automation like lead routing and reconciliation; and overnight micro-products and directories built by teams of agents. (The transcript has transcription errors, so I've kept to what's clearly stated.)

The second one is the one I'd start with. He calls it a "Trojan horse custom project demo". You give the model a company's website address, let it read the site, then ask it to build something useful for that business, such as a store locator, and send it over as your pitch. He also floats redesigning stale-looking local business websites and charging $50 to $100 a month. And he says agencies would charge roughly $10,000 to $100,000 for the kind of playable work in his first idea. Those are his claims, not mine, and I haven't verified the numbers.

He's also upfront about the model's weak spots: short answers unless you ask for length, some tired writing habits, confusing model tiers with no free option, and a built-in browser in Antigravity that he doesn't rate. The point is that the model matters less than the move. A faster, cheaper builder makes it cheap to show before you sell.

Why does a demo beat a proposal?

Because buyers of AI work are in the same position as that CEO. They can't judge what you'll build, they've been burned by hype, and they don't have time. A proposal is a promise. A demo is evidence.

I saw the same thing with an owner of an electrical contractor in Sydney. He didn't need convincing after the first test of his accounts chat surfaced an overdue payment nobody had flagged. That was his demo.

From those two stories and Greg's idea, I use a simple test. I call it the nine-second demo, and it has three parts:

  1. Their material. Use the buyer's own site, words, prices or documents. A demo on generic data proves you can use AI. A demo on their data proves you understand them.
  2. One job. Pick a single task they already do by hand: answering the same three questions, turning a site visit into a quote, summarising competitors. Not a platform. One job.
  3. One next step. End with a single, small ask: a 20-minute call, a test on their real data, a 49€-style diagnostic. Not a retainer.

If the buyer can't grasp it in about nine seconds, the demo is too big.

How do you build a nine-second demo? 5 moves for this week

  1. Pick one prospect. One business you'd genuinely like to work with, with a public website and an obvious pain. One, not twenty. Twenty shallow demos teach you nothing.
  2. Find the repeatable job. Read their site and look for work done again and again: the same enquiry, the same quote, the same report. Use the first prompt below to get a shortlist.
  3. Build the smallest version. A page, a chatbot answering from their own text, a one-page quote draft, a short video. Whatever shows the job done, in minutes rather than days.
  4. Check every fact. Use the second prompt below. Anything you can't trace to their material gets removed or marked as a guess. Never show a made-up price as if it were theirs.
  5. Send it with one ask. Two lines: what you made, what it's based on, and one small next step. Say clearly that it's a draft built only from public information.

Prompt 1: find the repeatable job

Paste a prospect's website text 
You are an AI consultant preparing a short demo for a business. Below is the text of their public website.

[paste the pages: home, services, pricing, contact, FAQ]

1. In two sentences, tell me what this business sells and to whom.
2. List 5 tasks they probably repeat every week (answering the same questions, quoting, scheduling, reporting, follow-ups). For each, name the evidence on the site that suggests it.
3. Pick the ONE task where a small demo would be most convincing in under a minute. Explain why.
4. Describe the demo in 5 lines: what the owner sees, what it uses from their site, and what it does NOT know.
5. List anything I would need to ask them before this could run for real.

Prompt 2: make it show its evidence

Run before you send anything 
Review the demo I'm about to send to a prospect. Their original website text and my demo are below.

[paste the website text]
[paste or describe the demo and its output]

For every fact, price, claim or promise in my demo, make a table with three columns: the claim, the exact sentence on their site that supports it, and a status (Supported, Guessed, or Missing). Then rewrite anything marked Guessed or Missing so it is either removed or clearly labelled as an assumption. Finish with the three things most likely to embarrass me in front of this owner.

How do you do this without being creepy or getting it wrong?

A demo built from someone's website is a gift only when it's honest. Four rules I'd keep:

  • Public material only. Their website and what they've published. Nothing from a private source, and no personal data.
  • Label it as a draft. "I built this from your public site in an afternoon. Here's what it gets right and what it would need from you."
  • Don't invent. If the site has no price, the demo has no price. A guessed number in a pitch destroys trust faster than no demo.
  • Take it down if they say no. A demo is an invitation, not a trap.

The same discipline applies on the other side of the table. When I teach clients to use AI on their own business, the first win is never the clever one. It's the one where the AI shows its evidence.

If you're the buyer: how do you ask for a demo?

If you're a business owner hiring an AI coach, freelancer or agency, this idea works for you too. Before you pay for a proposal, ask: "Show me one small thing, using my own material." A good consultant will be glad to. A reluctant one is telling you something.

Ask for three things. A demo that uses your real words and prices. A plain list of what it does not know. And what it would take to run it for real on your data. If the answer to the second question is "nothing", be careful.

Is this an opportunity for AI consultants and freelancers?

Yes, and I'd argue it's the best entry point for a new one. Most people selling AI services start by pitching: slides, a list of capabilities, a call. Everyone else is doing the same, so you're one voice among many.

A demo changes the conversation. Instead of "I can help you with AI", you say "I looked at how you answer enquiries, and here's a first version of the answer sheet". You've shown that you read their business before asking for money.

Two cautions from my own experience with clients. First, don't price the demo itself as the product. Greg's $50 to $100 a month figure for website redesigns is his idea, and I'd treat it as a starting point for a conversation, not a business model. Second, keep the next step small and paid: a diagnostic where you map the work, rather than a free build that becomes unpaid work. The demo earns the conversation. The diagnostic earns the engagement.

A proposal asks for trust. A demo earns it in nine seconds.

FAQ

What is a demo-first pitch?

Instead of sending a proposal, you build a small working example from the prospect's own public material, such as a page, a draft quote or a short video, and send that with one clear next step. They judge your work by seeing it.

What did Greg Isenberg say about Gemini 4 Neon?

In a 16-minute solo episode of The Startup Ideas Podcast, published on 20 November 2026, he calls it underrated and lists five ways to make money with it, including custom demos sent to companies as a pitch. The transcript says Google sponsored the episode.

Do I need Gemini 4 Neon to do this?

No. The idea works with any capable AI assistant that can read a website and build a simple page, document or tool. The model is the means; the demo-first move is the point.

Is it okay to build a demo from a company's website without asking?

Using only what they have published is a reasonable starting point, but label the demo as a draft built from public information, don't invent prices or claims, and remove it if they ask. If in doubt, ask first.

What makes a good demo for a small business?

It uses their own words and numbers, solves one task they already do by hand, can be understood in about nine seconds, and ends with one small next step rather than a large commitment.

How do I check that an AI demo is accurate?

Ask the AI to list every claim, price and promise in it next to the exact sentence from the source that supports it, and to mark each as supported, guessed or missing. Remove or label anything it can't trace back.
